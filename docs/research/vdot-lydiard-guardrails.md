# VDOT, Lydiard, and the volume cap

**Date:** 14 October 2026

**Question this note answers:** what each named system actually requires, what the injury research does with a week-over-week cap of about 10%, and where those sources are silent for a finish-line runner, a performance runner, and a longevity runner. It does not choose a guardrail for Running Tutor.

**How claims are labeled.** A claim below is either from the person or organization that owns it, or from a peer-reviewed paper. The coach library at `/Users/oleksandrmorozov/www/running-tutor` is **local prior art**. Nothing in that library, including its prices and its "science-backed" wording, is treated as external evidence. Where a primary text could not be read, the gap is stated.

Pace tables from Daniels, the Cooper distance-to-VO2 lookup, and Lydiard book prose are not reproduced.

## 1. Jack Daniels' VDOT

### What the number is

In a 1 December 2005 article, Jack Daniels, Ph.D., describes VDOT as a **pseudo-VO2max based strictly on race performance**. He and Jimmy Gilbert put runners of equal performance on one economy curve, so those runners share one mathematically generated value. Two runners with the same race result share a VDOT even when the laboratory VO2max each would show is different: one may have high economy and a lower laboratory value, the other the reverse. Daniels states that a race also folds in motivation and willingness to tolerate discomfort, which a laboratory test does not. The name is his shortening of "V-dot-O2max," the rate of oxygen uptake.

He says the tables, and the formulas used to build them, have been in use since the 1970s. The detailed tables are in *Oxygen Power*. A Google Books record gives that book as J. Daniels and J. Gilbert, *Oxygen Power: Performance Tables for Distance Runners* (1979, 107 pages). A 2016 trial protocol cites the same title as a 1979 Cortland, New York, printing. A publications list hosted elsewhere dates a Cortland printing to 1973. Daniels' 2005 article names the book and does not give a year. **The printing year is not settled here.**

The 2005 article says formulas exist that turn a performance into a VDOT and a VDOT into a training pace. It does not print the coefficients. This note does not copy coefficients from calculators or from student theses that claim to reprint the book.

### Inputs

Daniels' published procedure, in that article:

- A **recent race time** at a known distance.
- The race used for the number should be on a **flat course, with good footing and good weather**. A difficult course or bad weather lowers the VDOT relative to what the same fitness would score in good conditions.
- If several recent distances are available, **the highest VDOT** is the one he says describes current fitness, and it is the one used for training intensity.
- If the athlete will also train on the same hard course (his example is cross country), he says it is fair to use the VDOT from that course for sessions on that kind of course.
- A VDOT "can be generated for virtually any performance over any race distance," and *Oxygen Power* covers more than 40 distances. He says the value is **most accurate for races lasting from about 3½ minutes to about 3½ hours**.
- The condensed table in the article is indexed by these distances: 1500 m, mile, 3000 m, 2 mile, 5000 m, 10,000 m, 15 km, half marathon, marathon.

He uses the word "recent" and does not define it as a number of weeks. **A numeric recency window was not found in a Daniels text read for this note.**

The current V.O2 calculator (vdoto2.com), the product that publishes this method, asks for "a recent or estimated race result." That is wider than the 2005 article, which describes a race that was run. The same site's 10 March 2022 support note repeats the highest-VDOT rule when several results disagree, and it treats course and weather the same way Daniels does.

On 3 June 2018 the V.O2 site published answers attributed to Daniels. Asked how to raise VDOT with no race, the answer marked as his is: if training is going well without undue stress, raise VDOT by **one unit about every 4 to 6 weeks, and not more often**. That rule updates an existing score. It does not say how old a race may be.

### Outputs

From the 2005 article, one VDOT then yields:

- **Equivalent performances** at other distances. Daniels says the tables can relate a known performance to performances at other distances, and that a prediction is accurate only when the conditions of the two efforts are similar. He gives the example that a cool half marathon does not accurately predict a hot marathon.
- **Training paces** at fractions of that VDOT. He names interval pace (around the speed of a race lasting about 10 to 12 minutes), threshold pace, easy and long-run pace, and marathon pace.

The V.O2 calculator page states the current pace set and the bands it publishes (percent of VO2max, and a heart-rate band). Sample workouts on that page are not copied here.

| Pace on the calculator | Intensity the page states | Role the page states |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | 59–74% of VO2max, or 65–79% of HRmax | Warm-up, cool-down, recovery, and ordinary long runs. Conversational. The page allows the pace to sit up to 20 seconds per mile off the specified pace on a given day. |
| Marathon | 75–84% of VO2max, or 80–90% of HRmax | Race-pace practice for a marathon, or an alternative to easy pace on a beginner's long run. |
| Threshold | 83–88% of VO2max, or 88–92% of HRmax | Comfortably hard tempo or cruise intervals, to improve endurance. |
| Interval | 97–100% of VO2max, or 98–100% of HRmax | Aerobic power. Work bouts of about 3–5 minutes, not all-out. The page likens the pace to what could be held for about 10–12 minutes in a race. |
| Repetition | No percent band on the fetched page | Speed and economy. Short and fast, with recoveries long enough that each repeat feels no harder than the last. Likened to current 1500 m or mile race pace. |
| Fast reps | No percent band on the fetched page | 800 m race pace, to stress anaerobic power. Work bouts no longer than 90 seconds, not faster than the athlete's most recent 800 m. |

The 2022 support note calls the cross-distance figures **physiological equivalent performances, not predictions**. That is a narrower claim than the 2005 article's "predict." Both statements are from the VDOT publishers. A later decision has to notice the difference: the article allows a prediction when conditions match; the 2022 note refuses the word "prediction."

### What VDOT does not contain

The VDOT procedure read here is a way to score a performance and to pick paces. It is not a season plan and it is not a weekly mileage rule. Daniels' separate 2018 answers, on the same site, do describe how he likes to open a program: about **six weeks of easy running** (stride rate and a 2-2 breathing rhythm), then a **repetition** phase, then **interval and threshold**, with some repetitions kept in so speed and economy do not drop. He also says that, absent soreness, injury, or feeling depressed, more mileage probably still helps, and that the increase should stop when the athlete feels more tired or stressed. Those are coaching answers. They are not the VDOT calculation, and they do not state a 10% cap.

## 2. The 12-minute Cooper run

**Owning paper:** Kenneth H. Cooper, "A Means of Assessing Maximal Oxygen Intake: Correlation Between Field and Treadmill Testing," *JAMA* 203, no. 3 (1968): 201–204, doi:10.1001/jama.1968.03140030033008, PMID 5694044. The equation and category bounds below were read from a PDF of that article. The open JAMA page used here exposes the abstract, not the tables.

### What it measures

It estimates **laboratory maximal oxygen consumption** (VO2max, ml/kg/min) from how far a person can go in 12 minutes. Cooper's claim is that the test measures cardiovascular fitness. The abstract says the correlation with treadmill VO2max was high enough to estimate maximal oxygen consumption from the field test alone, and that the test was a better fitness indicator, in this study, than a 600-yard run.

### Who was tested, and how

- **115 US Air Force men** (officers and airmen). Age 17–52, average 22. Mean height 69.7 in, mean weight 168 lb.
- Flat, measured 1-mile hard course. Longest possible distance in 12 minutes. **Running preferred; walking allowed** when needed to avoid exhaustion. Not sooner than two hours after eating. Each man did the field test at least twice. Treadmill testing followed within three days.
- Conditions during the study: about 54–74°F, humidity about 54–93%.
- Treadmill VO2max used a protocol built from Taylor, Buskirk, and Henschel and from Mitchell, Sproule, and Chapman, with expired air collected in the last two minutes of several three-minute stages.

### The formula he printed

He regressed **distance on VO2max**, not the other way around, because motivation moves the field result more than it moves the laboratory value.

**Distance in miles = 0.3138 + 0.0278 × VO2max (ml/kg/min).**

Correlation **r = 0.897**, residual variance reported as 0.00985, df = 113. The units are fixed by his Table 1: at 1.50 miles the table lists 42.6 ml/kg/min, which is what the equation returns. He did not print the algebraically inverted form (VO2max solved from distance). A kilometre rearrangement that appears in later papers is not his printed equation. He did publish Table 1 so a 12-minute distance can be read as an estimated VO2max. That table is not copied here.

He reports **more scatter below 1.40 miles**. Dropping everyone at or under 1.40 miles did not meaningfully change the slope. The table footnote says data below 1.0 mile were insufficient for a reliable comparison. He also says most of the 115 were under 25, so accuracy at older ages is hard to claim from this sample; his preliminary note is that people up to at least 50 still sat near the same line. **Women were not in the sample.**

Table 2, his fitness bands for this test:

| 12-minute distance | Estimated VO2max (ml/kg/min) | Fitness level |
|---|---|---|
| under 1.0 mile | under 25.0 | Very poor |
| 1.0 to 1.24 miles | 25.0–33.7 | Poor |
| 1.25 to 1.49 miles | 33.8–42.5 | Fair |
| 1.50 to 1.74 miles | 42.6–51.5 | Good |
| 1.75 miles or more | 51.6 or more | Excellent |

He describes these bands as a slight modification of ratings Bruno Balke had suggested. The test is a fitness measurement. It is not a race, and the paper prescribes no training paces and no weekly mileage rule.

A separate reliability note in the paper: Bigbee and Doolittle, in a written communication Cooper cites, retested 149 junior-high boys four days apart and got a reliability coefficient of 0.976. That is a different population, and only nine of them had VO2 measured, on a bicycle.

## 3. Can a Cooper result stand in for a race time?

**Daniels does not say to do this.** No Daniels text read for this note mentions the Cooper test.

Two different substitutions get conflated, and only one is even the same kind of input:

1. **Put Cooper's estimated ml/kg/min into the slot where a VDOT number goes.** Daniels' definition blocks this. VDOT is the performance score. Cooper's number is an estimate of the laboratory value Daniels says VDOT is not. The two can share units and still name different things.
2. **Treat "this far in 12:00" as a race performance and run it through the VDOT method.** Twelve minutes sits inside the 3½-minute to 3½-hour window Daniels calls the accurate range, and he says a VDOT can be generated for virtually any distance. His published procedure, though, is a **time for a standard race distance**, looked up on the tables, under the course and weather rules above. The calculator's documented input is a race result. **A Daniels instruction that a 12-minute walk-run is an acceptable race entry was not found.**

One later research protocol does make substitution (2), and it should not be read as Daniels' method. The Run Clever trial design (*BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders* 17 (2016): 177, doi:10.1186/s12891-016-1020-0; Rasmus Oestergaard Nielsen is a coauthor) lets each participant pick one of three field tests: a 6-minute run, **Cooper's 12-minute run**, or a 5 km run. The result is then turned into paces with "the V.O2 (VDOT) values presented by Daniels." The design paper cites *Oxygen Power* and the 2nd edition of *Daniels' Running Formula* (Human Kinetics, 2005). It does not print the conversion. It also collapses intensity into three bands of its own (easy about 50–80% of the estimated VO2max, moderate about 81–87%, hard about 88–100%), which are not the Easy / Marathon / Threshold / Interval / Repetition bands on the V.O2 calculator.

So: Cooper's own formula estimates laboratory VO2max. It does not, in his paper, estimate VDOT. A 2016 trial used a Cooper distance as one allowed performance test feeding Daniels' tables. The owners of the two methods do not describe that bridge.

## 4. Arthur Lydiard's periodization

Lydiard's books were not copied and were not used as the text. The phase order and the mileage finding below come from two documents that present his own instruction:

- An edited transcript of his April 1990 seminar in Osaka, transcribed by Nobuya Hashizume and published in *American Track & Field* (the copy read is the PDF "Arthur Lydiard's Lecture"). Hashizume's footnotes are his, not Lydiard's, and they are not used as Lydiard's claims.
- *Arthur Lydiard's Athletic Training*, a guide for the 1999 Brooks / American Track & Field lecture tour, titled as by Arthur Lydiard and prepared by Hashizume and Steve Bobenhouse. It is a compilation for that tour, not a facsimile of a book.

The Lydiard Foundation's "About" page lists six phases: aerobic conditioning, hill strength, anaerobic development, integration, taper, racing. That is the foundation's naming. The 1990 lecture uses **coordination** and **freshen-up**, not "integration" and "taper." The lecture is the source for the order below.

### Who it was built for

The method described in the lecture was built for **competitive runners preparing for an important race**, across middle distance and distance. The athletes he names as the proof are Peter Snell (800 m and 1500 m), Murray Halberg (5000 m), Barry Magee (marathon), and John Davies (1500 m). He says middle-distance and distance runners need the same things up to the last part of the year: a high oxygen uptake, speed, and anaerobic development. He used **one conditioning schedule until about the last 10 weeks**, then chose the event and adjusted. The schedule is written by **counting backward from the first important race**.

The 1999 guide also has a separate **"For Joggers"** section aimed at general cardiac efficiency. That section is not the race pyramid. It is the part of these documents that speaks to someone who is not yet racing.

### Phases and order

Forward order, from the lecture's "How to set up a training schedule." Durations are reserved from the race date backward; conditioning gets whatever time remains.

1. **Marathon conditioning** (aerobic). Hopefully **10–12 weeks**. Starts with aerobic running only. Then adds a day of easy fartlek and strong runs of about 5 km and 10 km (in the guide's schedule notes: the 10 km about once every two weeks, the 5 km more often).
2. **Hill resistance.** **4 to 6 weeks.** Hill training two or three days a week, one long aerobic run, other days easy aerobic running or leg-speed work. The lecture describes the hills as resistance: knee lift, bounding, and ankle work, using body weight. He says there is no need to sprint the climbs; slower climbing adds resistance. Downhill striding is for relaxed leg speed. In the 1990 lecture this block sits **after** conditioning and **before** the hard anaerobic track block.
3. **Anaerobic development.** **Four weeks** (he also says about 3½ to 4 weeks of hard anaerobic work, three days a week, reaches near the maximum he believes anaerobic capacity can reach). Repetitions or fartlek two or three days a week, one long aerobic run, other days sprint training or easy running. He tells the athlete to stop the session when the physiological reaction is achieved, rather than at a coach's round number of repeats.
4. **Coordination.** About **4½ to 5 weeks**, or, in the same schedule, six weeks if freshen-up is counted inside it, or four to five weeks of coordination proper. Sharpeners (short sprints with floats), under-distance and over-distance development races, fast relaxed running. The point he states is to accustom the body to running a given distance in a given time. Early in these weeks he has the athlete trial the race distance near race speed, without being shown the watch.
5. **Freshen-up.** **One week to ten days**, depending on the event. Fresh and sharp. Hard training is finished; racing is the anaerobic work.
6. **Racing.**

He is explicit that the day aerobic conditioning stops and anaerobic work starts, the aerobic base for that season is the one the athlete will race on. Further anaerobic work does not, in his account, raise that ceiling. Too much anaerobic work pulls the condition down; sharpeners are his way to keep the anaerobic gain without that cost.

### Training load at the start

**The race schedule does not assume a fixed weekly distance on day one.**

From the 1990 lecture, the load he says produced the best results, for the group that included Snell and Davies, was about **100 miles (160 km) a week in the main sessions**, plus an easy morning jog on six mornings, **no more than an hour**. He says that when he himself ran about **250 miles (400 km) a week** to test the idea, it was too much: athletes tightened up. The 100-mile week is what he reports for runners already inside that main conditioning, including his middle-distance runners. It is not stated as the first week of a beginner.

From the 1999 guide's conditioning chapter, in the first person it attributes to him:

- Build by **time**, not by a required mileage. Get able to run long continuously. Use out-and-back runs. Increase daily time as fitness allows.
- **Do not step straight onto the full schedule.** Work up to it according to fitness and ability.
- A two-hour continuous run, without problems, is the point at which he says to start running measured courses against the watch.
- The long weekly pattern he describes (on the order of one to two hours on ordinary days, and two hours or more on the long day) is an **aim**, and a later mile-based week of about 100 miles is "only as a guide," to be adjusted for program, fitness, and age.
- He places **no set ceiling** on extra miles run easier than the faster aerobic runs ("the more the better," provided those extra miles stay easy). The 250-mile experiment is the upper end he reports as having failed.

**No week-over-week percentage cap appears in these documents.**

The jogger section, same 1999 guide: run easily for several months; a novice should not strive hard for **at least six weeks**; someone used to about 15 minutes a day should lengthen every third day toward 30 minutes, then toward an hour, with two or three longer runs in the week. When an hour is comfortable, he says fun runs of an aerobic kind are reasonable. Anaerobic training enters **if racing is contemplated**. The section's stated purpose is cardiac efficiency, with jogging described as an aerobic way to develop the circulation.

## 5. A week-over-week cap of about 10%

### Where the number enters the research record

The earliest peer-reviewed statement of the number found for this note is a **clinical recommendation**, not a trial result.

Johnston, Taunton, Lloyd-Smith, and McKenzie, "Preventing running injuries: practical approach for family doctors," *Canadian Family Physician* 49 (2003): 1101–1109, PMID 14526862. In the training-methods section they write that, to minimize injury risk, **they recommend increasing training duration or intensity by no more than 10% per week**, and they grade that sentence **level III evidence** (their scale: expert opinion). A second sentence gives the same 10% weekly cap to **time spent on a new running surface**, again level III.

In the same section they:

- Quote Yeung and Yeung's 2001 review of prevention trials as concluding that the data then available could not support an optimal training load (Johnston marks that conclusion level I).
- Say outright: **"There are no studies of injuries among runners wanting to increase their distance."** Those runners, they say, should still progress gradually, and that sentence is also level III.
- Attribute the "too much, too soon" idea to earlier clinical papers, and say typical training programs come from a coaching principle, level III.
- Describe their own walk-run introduction (a fixed 30-minute session, three days a week, with the running portion lengthened by 30 seconds a session until the person can run 30 minutes). That table is a walk-run introduction. It is not a demonstration that their 10% sentence was derived from a mileage experiment.

They do not cite a study that found 10% to be the injury threshold, and they do not cite Joan Ullyot or Joe Henderson for the number.

**A primary text by Ullyot or Henderson that states a 10% mileage-increase rule was not found.** Magazine articles attribute the popularization to Ullyot's *Women's Running* (1976) and to Henderson in the 1980s. That attribution was not confirmed from either author's own text. An interview with Ullyot discusses weekly mileage in her books (about 70 miles, later revised toward 50) and does not mention the rule. A 2015 Henderson post uses "10 percent" for the share of weekly miles that is racing or speed work, which is a different claim.

So the number, as a citable research claim, arrives as **expert advice about duration or intensity (and, separately, about new surfaces)**. Later papers treat "the 10% rule" as a cap on weekly running volume. That narrowing is the later literature's, not a result printed by Johnston.

### What the trials and reviews do with it

**Buist and colleagues, GRONORUN, the randomized test of a program built on the rule.**

- Design: *BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders* 8 (2007): 24. The intervention is a 13-week program whose running **time** rises by 10% a week, in novices preparing for a four-mile recreational event. Both arms start from the same walk-run point. The design paper cites Johnston 2003 as the reference for the rule, and states that no study had yet tested it.
- Results: Buist et al., *American Journal of Sports Medicine* 36, no. 1 (2008): 33–39, doi:10.1177/0363546507307505. **532** novice runners, ages 18–65, no lower-extremity injury in the prior three months, **no running in the previous 12 months**. Graded 13-week program versus a standard 8-week program. Three sessions a week, comfortable conversational pace. Injury: a musculoskeletal complaint of the lower extremity or back that restricted running for at least one week.
- **Injury incidence was 20.8% (52/250) in the graded arm and 20.3% (48/236) in the standard arm** (χ² p = 0.90). Cox regression adjusted for BMI: OR 0.8, 95% CI 0.6–1.3. The authors reject their own hypothesis that more time to adapt would lower injuries. They note the two programs may have been too similar in weekly minutes, that intensity was not measured, and that weekly frequency was the same.

Nielsen and colleagues, reviewing that trial in 2012, calculate the graded arm's weekly duration increase as **10.5%** and the standard arm's as **23.7%**, and they note that **both sit above 10%**. The trial therefore does not compare a true under-10% progression with a much larger one. Damsted's 2018 review describes the same contrast as about 10% versus about 24% average weekly volume, HR 0.8 (95% CI 0.6–1.3).

**Nielsen and colleagues, 2014, the cohort that looked for a threshold.**

Nielsen et al., *Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy* 44, no. 10 (2014): 739–747, doi:10.2519/jospt.2014.5164. Exploratory one-year cohort inside the Danish Novice Running project. **873** novice runners with GPS watches, self-chosen running, no coach progression. After each session they classified the change from the previous seven days to the seven days before that:

- under 10%, or a regression
- 10% to 30%
- over 30%

The 10% cut was chosen because of the general belief, which they cite to Johnston. The 30% cut came from a small 2013 pilot (60 novice runners, 13 injuries) that Nielsen's own 2014 paper says must be read with extreme caution.

Results they report:

- **202** runners injured. **No statistically significant difference in overall injury rate** across the three exposure groups.
- For a subgroup they defined as distance-related (patellofemoral pain, iliotibial band syndrome, medial tibial stress syndrome, gluteus medius injury, greater trochanteric bursitis, tensor fascia latae injury, patellar tendinopathy), progressing **more than 30%** versus **under 10%** gave hazard ratio **1.59** (95% CI **0.96–2.66**, **p = 0.07**). That misses the p ≤ 0.05 line they set. The 10–30% band had very few of these injuries (they say n = 2–4), so comparisons involving that band are unstable.
- They write that it was **equally hazardous to rise 10–30% as to stay under 10%**, so the data did not show a dose response at the 10% line.
- They still say novice runners "may be well advised" to keep a two-week jump **under 30%**, and they warn that repeating a 25% jump for several weeks could still be a problem even though each step is under 30%. They ask for randomized trials.

**Reviews that come back to the rule**

- Nielsen et al., "Training errors and running related injuries: a systematic review," *International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy* 7, no. 1 (2012): 58–75, PMID 22389869. Across volume, duration, intensity, and frequency, results conflicted. They could not identify which training errors relate to injury. On the 10% rule specifically, they report the Buist trial's null result and say a fair test against a much steeper arm (they mention 40–60%) had not been done.
- Damsted, Glad, Nielsen, Sørensen, and Malisoux, *International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy* 13, no. 6 (2018): 931–942, PMID 30534459. Four eligible studies. Their conclusion: **very limited evidence** that changes in training load are associated with injury, and **no evidence for using the 10% rule** as the maximum weekly increase. **No well-defined hazardous threshold was established.** They also note that the runners studied had low absolute mileage, so a large percentage can be a small number of kilometres, and that this cannot be read across to a competitive runner at a much higher weekly distance (their example is 100 km a week).
- *Journal of Athletic Training* 57, no. 7 (2022): 650–671 (online 3 September 2021), doi:10.4085/1062-6050-0195.21, correspondence Jean-Francois Esculier. Thirty-six prospective studies or trials, 23,047 runners. Evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity, or recent changes in those, to injury was **conflicting**. Key point, in the review's words: the popular **10% rule for increasing weekly distance is not justified**, and no universal progression can be issued from the current evidence.

### What this body of research supports, and what it refuses

It supports these statements:

- By 2003, sports-medicine clinicians at one centre were advising patients not to raise **duration or intensity** more than 10% in a week, and they classed that advice as expert opinion. They also said the studies of people who want to run farther did not yet exist.
- A randomized trial in **novice** runners, built to apply that rule to a first four-mile buildup, **found no reduction in injury** against a shorter, steeper standard program.
- In novice runners choosing their own running, an overall injury difference at the 10% line was **not shown**. A signal, short of the authors' significance line and limited to one injury grouping, sits at **jumps above 30% across two weeks**, not at 10%.
- Systematic reviews through 2022 **refuse the 10% rule as an evidence-based cap** and refuse a single safe percentage.

It does not support:

- 10% as the threshold at which injury starts.
- 10% as a cap that protects trained runners, performance runners, or any cohort other than the novices in these studies.
- 10% as a rule about kilometres specifically, in the paper that first states the number. Johnston's sentence is duration or intensity.
- Any claim about race times, paces, or cardiovascular benefit. The outcome in these papers is injury.

## 6. Where the systems conflict, and where each is silent

The three runners, as this note uses them:

- **Finish-line:** first race, including a first 5K.
- **Performance:** training toward a target time.
- **Longevity:** cardiovascular health, no race.

### Conflicts between the systems

**The number each one produces.** Cooper's output is an estimate of laboratory VO2max in ml/kg/min, from a 12-minute walk-run, in the population above. Daniels' VDOT is a performance score he defines as not that laboratory value. Using one in place of the other is not a step either owner describes. Run Clever's 2016 design is a third party's bridge, and it does not publish the arithmetic.

**What a hard effort is for.** Daniels uses a recent race to set every training pace and to state equivalent performances. Lydiard, in the lecture, uses time trials late, in coordination, after the aerobic block, the hills, and the anaerobic block, so the athlete learns to run a distance in a time. Cooper uses a 12-minute maximal effort, walking allowed, as a fitness test. Those are three different jobs for a hard sustained effort.

**Order of the year.** Lydiard's race order is long aerobic conditioning, then hills, then anaerobic development, then coordination, then a freshen-up, then racing. Daniels' 2018 answer about opening a program is six weeks easy, then repetitions (which he says are faster than interval and threshold work), then interval and threshold. VDOT itself has no phases. The 10% literature has no phases. A weekly percentage cap and a seasonal phase order do not answer the same question, and nothing read here derives one from the other.

**Load.** Lydiard's competitive conditioning, once the athlete is up to it, is reported at about 100 miles a week in the main runs plus easy morning jogs, with no percentage cap, and with a warning from his own 250-mile experiment. The first weeks are a time-based buildup, not that mileage. Johnston's 10% is a weekly ceiling on duration or intensity, offered as opinion, and the novice trial of a 10% time progression did not lower injury. Daniels' 2018 mileage answer is "more helps until the athlete feels worse," with no percentage. These three load statements cannot be applied as one rule.

**Who has been measured.** Cooper 1968 is young military men. The 10% trials and the Nielsen cohort are novice runners, many of whom had not been running. Lydiard's 100-mile report is his competitive group, including 800 m runners. Damsted 2018 says the novice load data do not transfer to a high-mileage competitive week. Daniels' tables are built from race performances across a wide ability range; the 2005 article does not limit them to elites, and it does require a performance inside a wide duration window.

## 7. Sibling coach library (local prior art only)

Everything in this section describes `/Users/oleksandrmorozov/www/running-tutor` as that library stands. It is not evidence about Daniels, Cooper, Lydiard, Friel, or injury epidemiology. The spec's prices and hosting choices are ignored. The skill file's phrase "science-backed" is the library's own label, not a finding.

Files read: `CLAUDE.md`, `.claude/skills/SKILL.md`, `.claude/skills/marathon-preparation/` (profile, heart-rate zones, microcycle load, and the six mesocycle files), `.claude/agents/running-tutor-agent.md` (hard limits), and `SPEC.md` section 13.

**Periodization.** The library calls its plan **reverse linear periodization** over a **24-week** macrocycle of six **4-week** mesocycles, in this order:

1. M1 Strength — neuromuscular work, hills and strides.
2. M2 VO2max.
3. M3 Threshold.
4. M4 Aerobic base.
5. M5 Marathon-specific.
6. M6 Race-specific, including taper.

`SKILL.md` says M1 and M6 are for every athlete in this system, and that M2–M5 may be reordered or shortened. The stated reason for starting at the high-intensity end is that the athlete already has an aerobic foundation. That order is the reverse of the Lydiard lecture order (aerobic conditioning first). This note records the contrast. It does not rank the two.

**Week structure.** Five running days: Tuesday hard, Wednesday easy, Thursday hard, Saturday long, Sunday recovery. Two hard sessions and three easy sessions. Inside each mesocycle the load is a percent of the athlete's current sustainable weekly kilometres: week 1 at 60–70% (the formula uses 65%), week 2 at 70–80% (75%), week 3 at 80–90% (85%), week 4 at 50–60% (55%). The skill says intensity stays roughly constant across the first three weeks and the volume dial is what moves. Week 4 is marked non-negotiable recovery.

**The library's own "10%".** `02-microcycle-load-model.md` says never to increase total weekly volume by more than 10% **compared with the same week of the previous mesocycle**. That comparator is not "this week versus last week." The midpoint formula itself rises by about 15% from week 1 to week 2 and about 13% from week 2 to week 3. The library's 10% sentence and a Johnston-style week-over-week cap are different rules that happen to use the same number.

**Intensity.** Zones are heart-rate zones. The zone file attributes them to **Joe Friel's 7-zone model, adapted for running**, anchored to lactate-threshold heart rate. LTHR is defined there as the average heart rate of the **last 20 minutes of a 30-minute all-out test**. The file's bands, as percentages of LTHR: zone 1 under 85%, zone 2 at 85–89%, zone 3 at 90–94%, zone 4 at 95–99%, zone 5a at 100–102%, zone 5b at 103–106%, zone 5c above 106%. **Those percentages were not checked against a Friel publication.** `SPEC.md` section 13 computes zones in code with a different zone-1 ceiling (`round(LTHR × 0.85)` as the top of zone 1) than the skill file (`round(LTHR × 0.84)`). That is an internal mismatch.

**VDOT inside this library.** The profile skill lists recent race times as optional, for a "VDOT cross-check" from a 5K, 10K, half marathon, or marathon "via Jack Daniels table." The profile card has a VDOT line. Weekly prescriptions in the skill are in heart-rate zones and kilometres. Target marathon pace is goal time divided by 42.195. VDOT is not the pace engine.

**The 30 km refusal.** `CLAUDE.md`, the agent hard-limit list, the M1 readiness checklist, and `SPEC.md` section 13 all refuse to start this macrocycle below **30 km/week**. The spec throws `volume_too_low` and tells the athlete to run a 4-week volume base first. The spec also requires an LTHR and refuses generation when injury notes match sharp or acute pain. The profile skill, in the same library, uses a **different** figure: if current volume is **under 40 km/week**, recommend a 4-week base before M1. The library therefore contains both a 30 km hard stop and a 40 km caution.

**Who the files address.** Marathon preparation, five days a week, a named marathon, goal types finish / PR / BQ / podium. A first marathon is defaulted to "finish," with conservative load. Training age under two years steps hard-session intensity down by one zone. **No file read here defines a no-race longevity plan or a first-5K plan.**

- Lydiard's books. Phase names, order, audience, and the 100-mile report are from the 1990 lecture transcript and the 1999 tour guide, both edited by Hashizume. Where those two documents use different names from the Lydiard Foundation site, the lecture was preferred.
